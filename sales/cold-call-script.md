# Cold-call script: book the 15-minute demo

**The only goal of the call is a meeting.** Don't sell the product on the phone. You're selling
15 minutes on a video call, where the deck and the live demo do the work.

The structure borrows from in-home direct-sales training (warm opener, questions before pitch,
small yeses, a two-option close, always ask for referrals), rewritten for owners in the trades.
Every line is true; nothing here works if you have to fake it.

---

## Before you dial

- **When:** 7:00–8:30am (before the first job) or 4:00–6:00pm (driving home). Avoid lunch and Mondays before 10.
- **Posture:** stand up and smile while you talk. It sounds different, and owners decide in seconds.
- **Have open:** your lead sheet, your calendar with two real open slots, and a pen.
- **Your number:** say who you are clearly. Never hide caller ID or pretend to be a customer.
- **Pace:** slow and calm. Owners are used to fast, pushy sales calls; sounding relaxed is the pattern break.

---

## The call (about 60–90 seconds)

### 1. Opener: honest and disarming (10 seconds)

> "Hi, is this [Owner name]? … Hey [Name], this is [Your name]. I'm a local founder here in
> [Ames]. This is a cold call, so I'll be quick. Can I have 30 seconds, and then you can tell me
> to get lost?"

*Why it works:* saying "this is a cold call" up front is unexpected and honest, which lowers their
guard. Asking permission gets the first small yes, and offering them an out makes them feel in control,
so they usually give you the 30 seconds.

No owner name? "Hi, who am I speaking with? … Are you the owner?"

### 2. Reason for the call, about them (15 seconds)

> "I work with plumbing and HVAC companies around [Ames] on one thing: the calls that come in while
> you're on a job. Quick question: when you're under a sink or on a roof and the phone rings,
> what happens to that call?"

*Why it works:* a question instead of a pitch. It makes them picture a real moment from their own day.

**Then stop talking.** Let them answer, however long it takes.

### 3. Dig once (15 seconds)

Whatever they say, follow up once:

- "Voicemail" → *"Do most people actually leave one?"*
- "My wife/office answers" → *"What about evenings and weekends?"*
- "I call them back" → *"How often has someone already booked somebody else by then?"*
- "I answer everything" → *"Even on a job? Respect. How about when you're on another call?"*

*Why it works:* they describe the problem in their own words. People believe what they say
themselves far more than what you tell them.

### 4. Bridge: one sentence of what you do (10 seconds)

> "That's exactly what I built. When you can't pick up, the caller gets a text from your business
> number right away, or an AI receptionist takes the job details and texts you a summary. Your
> phone still rings first, so nothing changes about how you answer."

### 5. The ask: two choices, not yes/no (10 seconds)

> "Easier to show you than explain it on the phone. It's a 15-minute video call where I show you
> the real thing. Would [Tuesday morning] or [Thursday afternoon] work better?"

*Why it works:* choosing between two times feels easier than deciding whether to meet at all. Use
real slots in the next 1–3 days, so their interest doesn't cool off.

**Then stop talking.** Whoever speaks next usually loses. Count to five in your head if you have to.

### 6. Lock it in (15 seconds)

> "Perfect, [Thursday at 4:30]. What's the best email for the calendar invite? … I'll send it right
> after we hang up. Is it OK if I call you on this number if the link gives you trouble?"

Repeat the day and time back. Ask them to put it in their phone while you're on the line.

*Why it works:* a commitment they say out loud, plus a calendar entry, sharply cuts no-shows.

### 7. Referral ask, even if they say no (5 seconds)

> "Last thing: who's another owner you know who's always on a job and misses calls?"

## When they push back

Use the same pattern every time: **agree → ask one question → re-ask for the meeting with two times.**

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "I'm busy right now." | "Totally get it, you're working. That's actually why I'm calling. Can I grab 15 minutes later this week? [Day] or [day]?" |
| "Just send me some info." | "Happy to. What would you want to see in it? … Honestly, the 15-minute demo shows it better than a PDF. [Day] or [day]?" |
| "Not interested." | "Fair enough. Can I ask: is that because you don't miss many calls, or just not right now?" (If it's a real no, thank them and ask for a referral.) |
| "I already have an answering service." | "Nice, so you know the problem. What does it run you a month? … Mine starts at $29. Worth 15 minutes to compare?" |
| "How much is it?" | "Plans are $29 to $129 a month, no contract. Which one makes sense depends on how many calls you miss. That's what the 15 minutes figures out. [Day] or [day]?" |
| "Who is this again? How'd you get my number?" | "It's [Your name], a local founder. Your number's on your Google listing. I'm calling local trades businesses directly instead of buying ads." |
| "Is this a robot/AI?" | "Nope, real person, real nervous founder." (Light humor works.) |
| "Call me back later." | "Will do. When's a good time, early morning or end of day?" (Write it down and call exactly then.) |

**Two "no"s means no.** Thank them, ask for a referral, and mark them "follow up in 60 days." Don't
push past a clear no; owners in a small town talk.

## Gatekeeper (spouse, office manager)

> "Hi, this is [Your name], a local founder. I'm trying to reach [Owner] about the calls that come
> in when [he/she/they]'s out on jobs. Who usually handles that?"

Often the gatekeeper IS the person missing calls. Ask them the same question from step 2, and offer
the demo to both of them.

## Voicemail (20 seconds max)

> "Hi [Name], it's [Your name], a local founder in [Ames]. I help trades businesses catch the calls
> that come in while they're on a job. I'll try you again [Thursday morning], or you can reach me at
> [your number]. Again, [Your name], [your number]."

Leave at most two voicemails per business, about four days apart.

## Persuasion rules that keep it honest

| Principle | How you use it | Never |
|---|---|---|
| Small yeses | Permission opener, their own answers, picking a time | Trick questions |
| Two-option close | "Tuesday or Thursday?" | Assume a yes they didn't give |
| Liking | Local founder, relaxed tone, use their name | Fake rapport or fake shared history |
| Scarcity | "I'm personally onboarding my first 10 businesses" (only if true) | Fake deadlines |
| Social proof | None yet, so lean on "local founder." Add real customer quotes once you have them | Invent customers or results |
| Silence after the ask | Let them answer | Fill the silence with more pitch |

## Legal basics (US; not legal advice)

- Dial by hand. No auto-dialers or pre-recorded messages.
- Call business numbers during business hours (8am–9pm their time at the absolute outside).
- If anyone says "don't call me again," add them to your do-not-call list immediately and keep it.
- Don't send cold texts. Email a meeting confirmation only after they've agreed and given you their email.

## Track every call

Add these columns to the lead sheet: `date_called`, `reached` (owner / gatekeeper / voicemail),
`outcome` (meeting / call back / no / do-not-call), `meeting_time`, `email`, `referrals`, `notes`.

After 20 calls, look at which opener question got owners talking, and lead with that one.
Expect most dials to reach voicemail or a gatekeeper; your own numbers after a few dozen calls are
the benchmark that matters.
